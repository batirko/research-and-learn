# Why it matters for your goal

Monitors promise fewer inspections, and every inspection costs the colony a morning. You need to know what a monitor can see before you decide whether two hives justify one [inference].

# The short version

- A hive scale weighs the hive every hour and sends the numbers to an app.
- Weight rises when nectar comes in and drops when a swarm leaves.
- A scale can't see eggs, so it can't tell you whether the queen is laying.
- A monitor tells you when to open a hive. It doesn't replace opening it.
- Prices change monthly, so date every price you read.

# Explanation: the problem

## An inspection is the only window, and each one costs the colony {medium}

Before monitors, a beekeeper learned what a colony did only by opening it. [[P01]] explains how much an inspection disturbs: the colony spends the rest of the morning restoring its warmth and order. So a beginner who inspects too often slows the colony down, and one who inspects too rarely misses a swarm.

**For your goal.** The question a monitor answers is when to open the hive.

# Explanation: the solutions

## A scale sees the nectar flow and the swarm {medium}

A hive scale sits under the hive and logs its weight. A steady daily gain means a nectar flow, such as oilseed rape in spring. In this guide, OSR stands for oilseed rape. A sudden drop of a kilogram or more in one afternoon usually means a swarm left. Neither number shows the brood, so [[B01]]'s first question, whether the queen is laying, still needs your eyes.

**For your goal.** A drop on the scale is a reason to open the hive that day.

## Sensors and apps add temperature and sound {context}

Some monitors add a temperature probe in the brood nest, or a microphone. A brood nest holds about 35 °C when brood is present, so a falling temperature can mean the brood has stopped. Sound monitors claim to hear a colony preparing to swarm [unverified].
